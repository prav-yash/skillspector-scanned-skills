# Claim Rejections & Grievance / Ombudsman Process

*Last verified: August 2026. Escalation contact details (specific Ombudsman office addresses, current turnaround-time numbers) change — search for current details if the user needs to actually file something, rather than stating an address or timeline from memory.*

## Common reasons claims get rejected or reduced — explain in plain language

- **Non-disclosure at policy purchase** — insurer says the user didn't declare a pre-existing condition or habit (e.g., smoking) truthfully on the proposal form. (Note: after the PED moratorium period is served, this ground gets much harder for insurers to use — see `claims-and-irdai.md`.)
- **Waiting period not yet served** — most policies have an initial waiting period (commonly ~30 days, varies) for illness (not accidents), plus longer specific waits for named conditions (e.g., cataracts, hernia) and pre-existing diseases. Don't state exact durations with confidence — point to the policy.
- **Treatment falls under a permanent/standard exclusion** — e.g., cosmetic procedures, certain experimental treatments; standardized exclusion wording exists across insurers per IRDAI norms, but the specific list is in the policy.
- **Room rent / category capping** — if the policy has a sub-limit on room category and the user opted for a higher category, the insurer may apply *proportionate deduction* across the whole bill, not just the room charge — a common source of "why is my payout so much lower than my bill" confusion.
- **Claim form or document discrepancy** — mismatched details, missing reports, or bills not itemized properly.
- **Treatment deemed not "medically necessary" or done on an outpatient basis** where the policy requires 24-hour hospitalization (though day-care procedure lists have expanded significantly — worth checking if their specific procedure is a "day care" procedure under IRDAI's definitions).
- **Claim intimated too late** — most policies require notifying the insurer within a set window of admission/discharge.

Ask the user for the **specific rejection letter/reason code** if they have it — insurers are required to state the reason in writing, and the right next step depends heavily on what that reason actually is. Don't speculate broadly if they can share the actual wording.

## Escalation path — walk the user through this in order

1. **Ask the insurer for the rejection in writing**, citing the exact policy clause, if they haven't already. The user has a right to this.
2. **File a complaint with the insurer's own Grievance Redressal Officer (GRO)** — every insurer must have one; this is usually the fastest resolution route and is often a prerequisite before external escalation. Point the user to the insurer's website "Grievance" or "Contact Us" section for the GRO's contact details (don't invent an email address).
3. **If unresolved after the insurer's stated timeline (commonly 15 days, confirm against their acknowledgment) or the user is unsatisfied**, they can escalate to:
   - **IRDAI's Bima Bharosa / Integrated Grievance Management System (IGMS)** — the regulator's complaint portal, useful for tracking and pressure, though IRDAI itself doesn't adjudicate individual claim disputes the way the Ombudsman does.
   - **Insurance Ombudsman** — a free, quasi-judicial forum specifically for resolving disputes between policyholders and insurers, for claims up to a monetary threshold (confirm current threshold — it has changed over the years, so search rather than state a number confidently). The Ombudsman covers a specific jurisdiction by the policyholder's location — search for "Insurance Ombudsman [user's city/state]" to find the right office rather than guessing.
4. **Beyond the Ombudsman**, a user can pursue consumer courts (Consumer Protection Act) or civil courts, but that's a genuinely legal step — tell the user plainly this needs an actual lawyer at that point, don't try to advise on litigation strategy.

## Framing that helps

- Reassure without overpromising: explain the *process* clearly so the user feels in control, but don't predict whether their specific claim will succeed — that depends on facts and policy wording only they and the insurer have.
- If a user is frustrated or feels cheated, acknowledge that without inflaming it or assuming bad faith on the insurer's part — reduced/delayed claims are often genuinely due to sub-limits or documentation gaps, not always misconduct. Give them the real next step either way.
- Keep the tone practical and step-by-step; this is often someone dealing with a sick family member and a stack of paperwork, not an abstract question.
